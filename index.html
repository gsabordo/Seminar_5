Main takeaway

A good idea begins with the people who have the problem. Technology comes in only after the problem is clear.

Trend I noticed

Startups are pushing digital tools into sectors that are still mostly manual, and the DICT supports young innovators by raising awareness.

Best practice

Build a simple version, test it with real users, and improve it step by step instead of waiting for a perfect product.

Read the full narrative report

I used to think of innovation as something that comes from big companies. The session showed that challenges in a field like agriculture can become tech opportunities when someone takes the time to understand them and design a useful solution.

I can think of improvements that fit community projects. A simple mobile or web app could help local farmers see current market prices or keep records of their crops and expenses. Even a basic online form and spreadsheet could be a first version that is later improved. As a student, I can try these ideas in my capstone or in a barangay project.

Main takeaway

A startup starts by noticing a gap, something people need that is not yet well served, then building a system that fills it.

What changed for me

Startups do not have to be big apps or famous companies. They can start small and local, as long as the problem is real.

A skill to practice

Spotting problems is a skill. I can train it by paying attention to the daily difficulties of the people around me.

Read the full narrative report

I can apply these ideas by looking for gaps in our school and community and by building simple systems for them. For example, a small online system for a local store, a school office, or a barangay could save time and reduce paper work.

In my future projects, I will first talk to the people who will use the system before I start coding. This seminar gave me more confidence that I can turn my IT knowledge into something useful, and possibly into a startup idea, one step at a time.
